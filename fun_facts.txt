List of Fun Facts

Saltwater
	•	Cannonball jellyfish (or Stomolophus meleagris) are named for the cannonball-like shape of their bells, which allows them to be more streamline and fast similar to one too! Many jellyfish lives like these ones start off as immobile polyps on the sea floor that catch prey that passes by, which then develop into mobile ephyra that eventually grow up into medusae or adult jellyfish. Cannonball jellyfish can secrete a toxic slime out of their stinging cells that deters predators and stuns prey in a nearby radius!
	•	Ocellaris clownfish (or Amphiprion ocellaris, with ocellaris meaning “small eye” in Latin) are small reef fish that form a symbiotic relationship with sea anemones. Symbiotic relationships are relationships between different species where both parties benefit. In this case for example the clownfish gets shelter for it and its nest, while the anemone gets protected from its predators by the aggressively territorial clownfish! Clownfish have social hierarchies ruled by a single dominant female, and if she dies the next-in-line male starts developing into a female to replace her dominance!
	•	Cownose rays (or Rhinoptera bonasus) are small stingrays named for their cow snout-like fronts. These forward finny faces don’t have any breathing holes on them, and instead what looks like a cow’s nostrils are actually 2 extra finger-like fin flaps called cephalic lobes that help guide and handle prey into the rays mouth on the bottom! Their stinging barb on their tail can grow back if broken off like a fingernail!
	•	Red king crabs (or Paralithodes camtschaticus) are named for their large size, but despite their name aren’t true crabs but are another kind of decapod crustacean closely related to hermit crabs and squat lobsters. King crabs are another example of the enormous evolutionary phenomenon known as carcinization, which is where some animals (but mostly different kinds of crustaceans like the king crab) evolve a more crab-like body plan from one that wasn’t crab-like before, with shared traits like a tucked under tail for example, to better deal with the shared problem of predators picking their weak-point tail like to-go fast food! The red king crab is become rarer due to overfishing.
	•	Sand tiger sharks (or Carcharias taurus) are one of the many obscure sharks that are chill and want nothing to do with humans, in fact did you know less than 1% of shark species are known to cause shark attacks? Adults have teeth specialized for small sized demersal (or dwelling near the sea floor) prey. Before they are born these sharks hatch inside their mom’s womb to be later birthed, but first in order to further develop they must eat undeveloped eggs and fellow siblings! Gnarly!
	•	Potbellied sea horses (or Hippocampus abdominalis) are a vulnerable species of sea horse named for their potbellies. It is common knowledge that the male seahorse has a brood pouch that the female lays their eggs in for incubation. When the eggs hatch the male “births” the 300-700 eggs with a sneezing motion! But did you know that seahorses can change colour too?

Freshwater
	•	Axolotls (or Ambystoma mexicanum) are a critically endangered aquatic salamanders now native only to Lake Xochimilco in Mexico in the wild. Axolotls remain in their larval stage unmetamorphosed, spending their whole lives in freshwater. These adorable amphibians can regrow any limb they lose. Wild axolotls are brown, while the pink ones seen everywhere as pets are axolotl and tiger salamander hybrids!
	•	Largemouth bass (or Micropterus nigricans) are apex predatory black bass sunfish in their pond habitats, feeding on a large variety of animal prey from smaller fish to frogs, literally being a “big fish in a small pond” that the saying talks about! Like other sunfish, these bass are great fathers, guarding their eggs until they hatch and the newly hatched fry for a few weeks! Unfortunately, largemouth bass have been introduced in other regions invasively due to their popularity as a sports fish.
	•	Bluegills (or Lepomis macrochirus), affectionately called “sunnies” are diurnal (meaning active during the day, the opposite of nocturnal) pond sunfish that are named for the blue colouration around their gills. They also have colour changing cells called chromatophores that display 5-9 vertical bands in defence. Did you know that bluegills are good fathers? Protecting their nest until their fry hatch and protecting them for a week after they hatch?
	•	Black crappies (or Pomoxis nigromaculatus, with the genus name Pomoxis translating to sharp operculum in Latin, referring to the black and white crappies’ spined gill covers) are popular freshwater angling or sports fish people like to commonly go fishing for. They are named for their black spots and darker colouration when compared to the similar white crappie! Black crappies can lay up to 40000 eggs that the males protects before hatching!
	•	Longnose gars (or Lepisosteus osseus) are primitive fish that have existed since around 200 million years ago! These prehistoric predators are known to be speedy at catching prey in rivers, but did you know that when oxygen levels lower in swampy water these fish can use their buoyant swim bladder as a “lung” to breathe gulped air with? Longnose gar have long lives for freshwater fish, with the oldest one found being 39 years old!
	•	The lake/rock sturgeons (or Huso fulvescens) are threatened freshwater fish that are bottom feeders, which mean they feed on the bed or floor of lakes and rivers. They use their shovel-like snouts to stir up and dig around the dirt, search around with whisker-like organs called barbels, and then use their extendible sucker mouths to catch prey they dug up! Did you know many sturgeon species are endangered due to unsustainable caviar or edible fish egg harvesting practices?

Extra
	•	Peacock mantis shrimp (or Odontodactylus scyllarus) aren’t mantises nor shrimp, but their own kind of crustacean that are cousins to hermit crabs and pillbugs! Did you know that the peacock mantis shrimp can punch as hard and fast as a bullet to hunt prey? We installed bullet-proof glass to make sure it doesn’t escape!
	•	Fleshy sea pens (or Ptilosarcus gurneyi) are cnidarians, meaning they are related to jellyfish, sea anemones, and coral! Did you know like coral, these filter feeding sea pens are colonial animals, meaning that each pen is actually made up of multiple individual polyp pals!
	•	Giant green sea anemones (or Anthopleura xanthogrammica) are also cnidarians like jellyfish, sea pens, and coral. Did you know that sea anemones form symbiotic relationships between many other different animal species? The most famous one is between the clownfish, where the fish gets shelter in exchange for fiercely defending the anemone’s territory!
	•	Barnacles (subclass Cirripedia) are sessile filter feeding crustaceans, that’s right the are related crabs and lobsters! The mobile larva of barnacles search for the perfect spot to spend the rest of their life in, where they then attach using super strong head glue that not even acid can weaken!
	•	Starry flounders (or Platichthys stellatus) begin their lives as relatively normal looking fish larva with a typical eye placement, as they live more open sea lives in their childhood. As the young fish gets older its other eye slowly migrates to the other side of its face as it slowly metamorphoses into an adult that’ll live on the seafloor!
	•	There are species of mussel that live in either fresh (subclass Palaeoheterodonta) or salt water (subclass Pteriomorphia). Mussels are mollusks like squid and snails, and specifically in the shell-tastic bivalve family of mollusks like clams! Mother freshwater mussels bait fish with mucus-y lures before spraying their gills with babies. These young mussels then hitchhike on the fish upstream before falling off when ready, insuring the mussel population doesn’t keep going downstream! Many freshwater mussel species are endangered however.
	•	Coral (various Anthozoan subdivisions) are cnidarians like jellyfish, sea anemones, and sea pens, but are more closer to the latter two, forming the subphylum Anthozoa with them! They are colonial animals like sea pens, being made up of multiple individual filter feeding polyp pals that work together to build their collective calcium carbonate exoskeleton! Their cool colours come from vibrant algae on them that they form a symbiotic relationship with, protecting the algae in exchange for some of the algae’s solar energy!
	•	Did you know that red-bellied piranhas (or Pygocentrus nattereri) are overhyped by the movies? They are mainly vegetarian and only spring into their iconic feeding frenzy in times where food is scarce?
	•	Common cuttlefish (or Sepia officinalis) aren’t fish but mollusks like snails and clams, more specifically they belong in the tentacular cephalopod family of mollusks like squid! Did you know cuttlefish use their colour-changing expertise to confuse their preys’ eyes? Cephalopods change colour using microscopic cells called chromatophores, which act like inky waterballoon-y pixels on the surface of their skin!
	•	Goliath groupers (or Epinephelus itajara) are large predatory fish that live across saltwater oceans! The goliath groupers are the largest groupers in the world! With max lengths measuring up 2.5 meters long and weigh up to 363 kilograms! Goliath groupers have been reported in the 1950s making toddlers go missing by swallowing them whole!
	•	Pacific blood sea stars (or Henricia leviuscula) are echinoderms, related to sea urchins and sea cucumbers! Echinoderms are one of the most closely related invertebrate phylums to vertebrates like us! Sea stars can look with simple eyespots on the end of each of their arms, regrow lost arms, and eject their stomach from their mouth into their prey to capture and digest them! Wicked cool!
	•	Purple sea urchins (or Strongylocentrotus purpuratus) are also echinoderms, meaning they are related to sea stars and sea cucumbers! Sea urchins can move their many spikes around to move to their favourite food, algae and kelp! They eat with 5 self-sharpening teeth, and their mouthparts are called an Aristotle’s Lantern because he first described them as lantern-like!
	•	Orange-footed sea cucumbers (or Cucumaria frondosa) are echinoderms like sea stars and sea urchins! Named for their long bodies, sea cucumbers have some surprising visceral and versatile abilities! Sea cucumbers can either eject their respiratory system or other internal organs at potential predators, the former being used to tie up and ensnare them, while the latter distracts them. Sea cucumbers then later fire back these evisversted internals!
	•	Sessile tunicates (subphylum Tunicata) are stationary filter feeding invertebrates in their own tunicate phylum related to each other! Filter feeders are animals that filter water for small food pieces. Tunicates start their life as mobile, tadpole-like larva that search for the perfect spot to spend the rest of their lives in! Tunicate larva have notochords that they reabsorb when they metamorphose into adults. Notochords are the simplest form of a backbone, which allowed scientists to find out that the tunicate phylum is the invertebrate phylum most closely related to us vertebrates!
	•	Longspine snipefishes (or Macroramphosus scolopax) are named for the large spine on their dorsal fin and their elongated snout. They use these tubular mouths in a similar manner to sea horses, quickly gobbling up small crustaceans and other invertebrates. When they’re young fry they mainly eat small mysid shrimps, before diversifying their diet with other kinds of crustaceans when they grow up!
	•	The palette surgeonfish (or Paracanthurus hepatus) or blue tang goes by many names, including regal tang, hippo tang, royal blue tang, wedge-tailed tang, and flagtail surgeonfish! Like its other tang fish family members, it has venomous spines on its tail near its back to defend itself from predators. The scalpel-like shape of these spines is where surgeonfish get their name from!
	•	Red lionfish (or Pterois volitans) are slow moving venomous fish with bright warning colours that it uses to warn predators of the potent venom delivered by its dorsal spines. When an animal use bright colours to warn predators they aren’t worth eating it’s called Aposematism! Lionfish are native to the indo-pacific, but have become invasive in many other areas!
	•	Sea nettle jellyfish (genus Chrysaora) are one of the most commonly known jellyfish species, jelly fish are cnidarians like coral, sea anemones, and sea pens! Cnidarians are named for the special stinging cells called cnidocysts, which contain a coiled up nematocyst that acts like a hypodermic needle of venom when the cell makes contact with prey or predators! Many cnidarian cnidocyst stings aren’t strong enough to harm humans and are just for hunting prey, but sea nettles aren’t one of these and are named for their painful stings so keep your distance!
	•	The kelp family (order Laminariales) contains species that can live in both salt and fresh waters around the world. In their own family with other algae closely related to plants but technically aren’t plants themselves, these ornate organisms show that plants aren’t the only life on this planet that eats sunlight! The largest kelp species is the giant or bladder kelp, which grows so fast that it can grow 60 centimetres everyday and 45 metres every growing season! This allows giant kelp to form whole kelp forests that many marine creatures rely on for survival!
	•	Algae are a broad term that refers to many saltwater and freshwater groups of usually aquatic unicellular or single-cells photosynthetic organisms, that’s right plants aren’t the only photosynthesizers! Photosynthesis is the process where an organism takes carbon dioxide, water, and sunlight and turns it into sugar for energy and oxygen! Did you know that algae and other plankton in the ocean produce over 70% of our planet’s oxygen? That’s more than every tree in the world combined!
	•	Upside-down jellyfish (genus Cassiopea) rhythmically pulse to maintain their upside-down orientation against the sea floor in their seagrass and mangrove homes. Did you know that similar to coral, these cnidarians also use the help of symbiotic algae to provide them with the algae’s solar energy? Up to 90% of the jelly’s nutritional requirements come from these awesome algal allies!
	•	Lumpfish/lumpsuckers (family Cyclopteridae) are small fish named for their modified pelvic fins that evolved into adhesive discs that act as suction cups that allow them to hold onto a surfaces against currents to live a more stationary life, consuming small passing invertebrates. These funny fish are a big help in salmon aquaculture farms, as they help clean off parasitic sea lice crustaceans off of the salmon!
	•	American eels (or Anguilla rostrata) are true eels that live in freshwater habitats during their adulthoods. True eels like the American eel start their lives as transparent glass eel or leptocephalus larvae that were once thought to be their own species because of how different they were, but now we know these are true eels before metamorphosis! American eels specifically hatch in saltwater, before migrating to freshwater, before finally migrating back one last time to their secretive spawning grounds!
	•	American Lobsters (or Homarus americanus) can weigh over 20 kilograms, which makes them the heaviest crustacean in the world! Lobsters start the first few months of their life as planktonic or microscopic, free swimming larva that later grow up into a more benthic or sea floor dwelling lifestyle. Did you know that lobsters can come in many rare colour variations? With the rarest one being a 1 in 100 million albino or white lobster colour morph?
	•	Greater sirens (or Siren lacertina) are salamander amphibians like axolotls, and they basically look like axolotls or larval salamanders but longer and more worm-like, which helps them swim around in their murky river habitats. There are many species of salamanders like sirens and axolotls that live a whole unmetamorphosed life aquatically, being neotenic or maintaining the juvenile form. Greater sirens are nocturnal and feed on bivalve molluscs like freshwater mussels, sallowing them whole!
	•	Slipper lobsters (family Scyllaridae), affectionately called “mudbugs” are saltwater crustaceans like their namesakes that kind of look like a slipper or shoe! Slipper lobsters are durophages, meaning they exclusively feed on super hard-shelled or durable animals like mussels and other crustaceans, having adaptations like crushing arms for example for the job! They also serve as food for other durophages ironically!
